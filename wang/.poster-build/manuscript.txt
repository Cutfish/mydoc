Layered Heterostructural Thermochromic Composite Films with Temperature Visualization for Adaptive Thermal Management
ABSTRACT: Addressing the persistent challenge of synergizing dynamic photothermal modulation, real-time temperature visualization, and high-capacity latent heat storage, this study reports the development of a hierarchical, reversible thermochromic composite film (TC-RGY20@FPCM) designed for adaptive thermal management.
To overcome the inherent limitations of organic phase change materials (PCMs)—specifically leakage during phase transition and insufficient thermal conductivity—a flexible phase change membrane (FPCM) was engineered via a solution casting-chemical crosslinking strategy. This architecture utilizes polyethylene glycol 1000 (PEG1000) as the thermal storage core and Polyvinyl Alcohol (PVA) as the flexible matrix. The integration of carboxylated cellulose nanofibers (CNF-COOH) establishes a robust confining network, while carboxylated multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs-COOH) facilitate efficient thermally conductive pathways. The resulting FPCM exhibits a significant melting enthalpy (ΔHm) of 59.140 J/g, a tensile strength of 7.18 MPa, and an elongation at break of 167.79%. Critically, the membrane maintains high structural integrity with a leakage rate below 2.5% after 2 h at 80 ℃, demonstrating superior thermal storage capacity, mechanical flexibility, and reliable leakage resistance.
Complementing the storage layer, a gradient thermochromic layer was fabricated via electrospinning, incorporating thermochromic microcapsules (TCMs) with discrete transition temperatures (approx. 22, 28, and 33 ℃) into Polyvinylidene Fluoride (PVDF) nanofibers. Systematic optimization of morphology, mechanical properties, and optothermal responsiveness identified a 20 wt% TCMs loading (TC-RGY20) as the ideal balance between functionality and structural reliability. The TC-RGY20 layer undergoes a distinct, reversible color transition from "dark → green → yellow → white" within the 20–35 ℃ range. This transition facilitates a significant average visible-light reflectance modulation (ΔR) of 30.316%, enabling an efficient "low-temperature absorption/high-temperature reflection" optical switch.
Finally, an integrated "dense storage-porous regulation" heterostructural film was realized by integrating the TC-RGY20 and FPCM layers using an aqueous thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) binder via electro-atomization spraying and hot-pressing. Outdoor irradiation experiments demonstrate that the TC-RGY20@FPCM system achieves bi-directional temperature regulation through a synergistic mechanism of "fiber scattering/reflection, chromic absorption switching, and latent heat buffering." Under low irradiance (< 200 W·m-2), the film maintains a temperature 2–4 ℃ higher than the bare copper reference; conversely, under peak high irradiance (> 700 W·m-2), it achieves a temperature reduction (ΔT) of -12 to -17 ℃ relative to the substrate. This dual-mode responsiveness underscores the film's exceptional performance in self-adaptive thermal management.

Keywords: Flexible phase change materials; Reversible thermochromism; Self-adaptive thermal management; Electrospinning; Temperature visualization


INTRODUCTION
The convergence of rapid global industrialization, population expansion, and rising living standards has driven an exponential surge in energy consumption. This precipitous reliance on fossil fuels has not only triggered a global energy crisis but also catalyzed severe environmental pathologies, characterized by the greenhouse effect and the increasing frequency of extreme climatic events[1,2]. According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), the building sector accounts for approximately 30-40% of global end-use energy consumption and contributes a substantial fraction of carbon emissions, with more than half of this energy expenditure dedicated to maintaining indoor thermal comfort[3,4]. Conventional centralized thermal management systems, which indiscriminately regulate entire building volumes, inherently suffer from massive energy inefficiencies. Consequently, Personal Thermal Management (PTM) has emerged as a transformative energy-saving strategy[5]. By precisely modulating the hydrothermal state of the localized human microenvironment to maintain thermal comfort, PTM technology theoretically enables a drastic enhancement in energy utilization efficiency and reduces the energy consumption of the built environment[6].
Among the diverse technological pathways for PTM, Phase Change Materials (PCMs) have established themselves as premier media for thermal energy storage and thermal buffering, owing to their intrinsic capacity to absorb and release substantial latent heat at quasi-isothermal transition temperatures[7]. Within the class of organic PCMs, polyethylene glycol (PEG) has garnered significant attention due to its high latent heat enthalpy, non-toxicity, non-corrosiveness, and excellent biocompatibility. However, the practical deployment of pure PEG is severely constrained by its propensity for liquid-phase leakage during solid-liquid transitions[8,9]. To mitigate this, researchers have developed various shape-stabilization strategies employing porous carbon architectures or polymeric networks to restrict molecular mobility via physical adsorption or chemical crosslinking[10,11]. Notably, biomass-derived cellulose nanofibers (CNF), characterized by high aspect ratios, exceptional mechanical strength, and abundant surface functionalities amenable to modification, have been validated as ideal scaffolding materials for constructing flexible phase change composite membranes[12,13]. Furthermore, addressing the inherent low thermal conductivity ubiquitous in organic PCMs, the integration of high-thermal-conductivity fillers—such as carbon nanotubes (CNTs) or graphene—to establish efficient heat conduction networks is essential for optimizing their dynamic thermoregulatory performance[14-16].
Beyond leveraging latent heat for passive thermal buffering, modulating surface optical properties represents another highly efficient pathway for passive thermal management[17]. Ideally, personal thermal management (PTM) materials should possess environmental adaptability, capable of dynamically tuning their spectral characteristics in response to ambient temperature fluctuations[18,19]. However, conventional radiative cooling fabrics or photothermal heating coatings typically exhibit static optical profiles, rendering them ill-suited for dynamic thermal environments characterized by diurnal temperature variations or seasonal shifts[20]. To transcend the limitations of single-mode and static regulation, reversible Thermochromic Materials (TCMs) have been integrated into intelligent thermal management system designs. These materials undergo reversible chromatic transitions under specific thermal stimuli, thereby dynamically modulating their absorption and reflection behaviors across the visible and near-infrared spectra[21]. Among these, organic thermochromic microcapsules based on leuco dye-developer-solvent systems have garnered particular attention due to their tunable transition thresholds, rich chromatic responsiveness, and processing versatility. Furthermore, the solid-liquid phase transition of their internal solvent component contributes supplementary latent heat, theoretically enabling a "light-heat-latent heat" tripartite synergistic regulation[22-24]. Current research has successfully applied TCMs in textile finishing and polymer doping to realize functionalities such as smart windows and adaptive camouflage[25,26].
Nevertheless, the development of integrated thermal management systems that simultaneously encapsulate high-performance flexible phase change thermal storage, all-weather adaptive radiative modulation, and intuitive temperature visualization remains impeded by formidable challenges. Primarily, an intrinsic structural design conflict persists: the simplistic physical blending of TCMs with thermally conductive enhanced PCMs (typically utilizing dark-colored fillers) frequently results in the optical masking of thermochromic transitions, thereby negating visual functionality. Furthermore, reconciling the conflicting requirements for mechanical flexibility and high latent heat enthalpy within a single composite system proves exceptionally difficult[27,28]. Secondly, the strategic selection of matrix materials and fabrication protocols is paramount. Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) emerges as an exemplary substrate for radiative cooling, distinguished by its superior weatherability, chemical inertness, and high mid-infrared emissivity originating from the molecular vibrations of C-F bonds[29]. Complementarily, electrospinning technology facilitates the construction of membranes featuring high porosity and micro/nano-scale fibrous hierarchies; these structural features induce intense Mie scattering of incident solar radiation, thereby achieving ultra-high solar reflectance[30,31].
While recent advancements in multifunctional thermal management fabrics have been notable—exemplified by Luo et al.[32] developing smart textiles capable of dual-mode radiative cooling and solar heating, and Zhang et al.[33] constructing core-shell reversible thermochromic fibers—the seamless integration of efficient thermoregulation with intuitive temperature visualization remains a formidable challenge. Furthermore, strategies for enhancing optical responsiveness have been illuminated by Lang et al. [34] via transparency modulation of cellulose membranes and by Pan et al.[35] through microcapsule shell optimization. Synthesizing these findings with progress in spectrally selective textiles[36] and recent reviews on electrospun nanofibers for thermal management[37], a critical fabrication bottleneck emerges: achieving a robust interface between a fragile, porous nanofiber layer and a dense, flexible phase-change layer without compromising the intrinsic optical architecture of the nanofibers. Moreover, existing systems frequently suffer from a lack of intuitive visual feedback, depriving users of real-time situational awareness regarding material operational states or environmental thermal risks—a significant deficiency in scenarios such as extreme environment operations or medical monitoring[38–40]. Consequently, developing a hierarchical structural design that decouples, optimizes, and subsequently reintegrates internal thermal storage/conduction functions with external thermochromic regulation represents the pivotal pathway to resolving these issues.
Herein, we report the engineering of a novel hierarchically structured, reversible thermochromic, and temperature-visualizing adaptive thermal management composite membrane (TC-RGY@FPCM). The architectural rationale hinges on the synergistic integration of three distinct mechanisms: microstructural scattering, macroscopic chromatic absorption, and phase-change latent heat buffering. Initially, a flexible phase change membrane (FPCM) was fabricated via a melt-blending and solution-casting strategy. By employing polyethylene glycol 1000 (PEG1000) as the thermal energy storage core and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) as the flexible matrix, we incorporated carboxylated cellulose nanofibers (CNF-COOH) and carboxylated multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs-COOH) to function as structural reinforcement scaffolds and thermal conductive networks, respectively, thereby circumventing the intrinsic leakage and low thermal conductivity limitations of traditional PCMs. Subsequently, a gradient thermochromic PVDF nanofiber membrane (TC-RGY) loaded with TCMs (transition temperatures at ~22 °C, 28 °C, and 33 °C) was developed via electrospinning. This functional layer leverages the high porosity of PVDF nanofibers to achieve efficient scattering-based cooling at high temperatures, while exploiting the chromatic gradient of microcapsules to enable photothermal heating at low temperatures and full-range temperature visualization. Finally, monolithic integration was achieved through electrostatic atomization deposition of a diluted TPU binder followed by mild hot-pressing. We systematically characterized the material’s micromorphology, chemical structure, phase transition behavior, mechanical properties, and optical responsiveness, and further validated its adaptive regulatory efficacy under complex thermal environments through outdoor field testing.

Fabrication of Flexible Phase Change Membranes (FPCM)
The fabrication workflow for the FPCM is schematically illustrated in Figure 1. Initially, a rigorous pre-treatment protocol was employed to ensure the optimal exfoliation and homogeneous dispersion of the functional reinforcing phases. Specifically, CNF-COOH (3 wt%) and MWCNTs-COOH (1.5 wt%, stabilized with PVP) were separately subjected to an identical interval ultrasonication regimen under ice-bath conditions (80 W; 10 min active/5 min interval; 3 cycles). Subsequently, the composite matrix was constructed by blending molten PEG1000 (65 wt%) with an aqueous PVA solution at 60 °C. The pre-dispersed nanofiller suspensions and a glycerol plasticizer (3 wt%) were sequentially incorporated into the matrix under continuous agitation until a homogeneous precursor solution was achieved. On this basis, chemical crosslinking and solidification were performed. Glutaraldehyde (2 wt%) was introduced into the precursor, and the system temperature was lowered to 40 °C. The pH was then adjusted to 4.0-5.0 using 0.1 M HCl to initiate an acid-catalyzed crosslinking reaction, which was maintained isothermally for 20 min. Following the reaction, the solution underwent vacuum degassing to eliminate entrapped micro-voids and was cast onto a leveled substrate. The final FPCM was obtained via a gradient drying profile (thermal curing at 40 °C for 4 h, followed by vacuum drying at 60 °C for 3 h) and subsequent equilibration at room temperature.

Fabrication of TC-RGY Reversible Thermochromic Nanofiber Membranes
The fabrication protocol for the TC-RGY series of reversible thermochromic nanofiber membranes is illustrated in Figure 2. Initially, a homogeneous 12 wt% PVDF matrix solution was prepared by dissolving PVDF into a binary solvent system comprising N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and acetone (ACE) (65:35, w/w). Subsequently, three distinct thermochromic microcapsules (TCMs)—specifically Rose Red (Ttrans ≈ 22 °C), Peacock Green (Ttrans ≈ 28 °C), and Yellow (Ttrans ≈ 33 °C)—were gravimetrically proportioned at a specific mass ratio of 5:7:5. These TCMs were introduced into the matrix solution at gradient loading levels ranging from 10 to 50 wt% (relative to the PVDF mass) and subjected to vigorous agitation to yield functionalized spinning precursors. Finally, electrospinning was conducted under standardized processing parameters (applied voltage: 11.5 kV; tip-to-collector distance: 15 cm; feed rate: 0.3 mL·h⁻¹; 17G needle) and rigorously controlled environmental conditions (25 ± 3°C, 35 ± 5% RH) to ensure jet stability and fiber uniformity. The resulting composite nanofiber membranes were designated as TC-RGY10 through TC-RGY50, corresponding to their respective TCMs weight fractions.

Figure 2. Schematic illustration of the fabrication workflow for reversible thermochromic nanofiber membranes.
Fabrication of the TC-RGY20@FPCM Adaptive Thermochromic Composite Membrane
The assembly of the TC-RGY20@FPCM composite membrane employs an interfacial coupling strategy facilitated by electrostatic atomization spraying (electrospraying), as depicted in Figure 3. Initially, a waterborne thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) binder was diluted with deionized water at a 1:1 mass ratio to modulate solution viscosity and optimize atomization kinetics. Subsequently, a micron-scale, homogeneous adhesive interface was constructed by applying the diluted binder onto the pre-treated surfaces of both the FPCM phase-change layer and the TC-RGY20 thermochromic layer via reciprocating electrospraying (two cycles) under an applied voltage of 10 kV and a working distance of 15 cm. Finally, the functional layers were aligned face-to-face and laminated under a constant pressure of 0.05 MPa within a 40°C thermal field for 20 min. This curing process facilitated robust monolithic integration of the bilayer structure driven by solvent evaporation and interfacial physical interlocking.

Characterization and Performance Evaluation
The microstructural architectures of the samples were scrutinized using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM, Regulus 8230). Particular analytical emphasis was placed on elucidating the cross-sectional hierarchies and interfacial bonding integrity of both the TC-RGY nanofiber series and the TC-RGY20@FPCM adaptive composite membranes. To quantify fiber morphology, SEM micrographs were processed using ImageJ software; statistical diameter distributions were derived via the stochastic sampling of representative fibers across diverse, randomly selected fields of view.
Chemical structures were interrogated via Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet 5700, Thermo, USA) operating in Attenuated Total Reflection (ATR) mode. This spectroscopic analysis aimed to deconvolute potential intermolecular chemical interactions among the FPCM components, identify the characteristic functional moieties of the PVDF matrix, and validate the successful incorporation of Thermochromic Microcapsules (TCMs) within the nanofiber network.
Crystalline morphologies and phase compositions were characterized using an X-ray Diffractometer (XRD-6100, Shimadzu, Japan). Diffraction patterns were acquired at a scanning rate of 5°·min⁻¹ over a 2θ range of 5°-80°. These diffractograms were analyzed to deduce the crystal structure evolution and phase information of the materials.
Mechanical properties were quantified using a dual-column universal testing machine (INSTRON-3365). Rectangular specimens with dimensions of 6 mm × 30 mm were subjected to uniaxial tension at a crosshead speed of 20 mm·min⁻¹. Stress-strain curves were recorded to derive critical mechanical parameters, including tensile strength and elongation at break.
Phase transition thermodynamics of the FPCM and TC-RGY nanofiber series were investigated using Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC250). Measurements were conducted under a protective nitrogen atmosphere (flow rate: 20 mL·min⁻¹) across a temperature range of -15~60 ℃. Heating and cooling scans were performed at a constant rate of 10 ℃·min⁻¹ to extract key thermal parameters, specifically phase transition temperatures and enthalpies.
Thermal stability and decomposition behaviors were characterized via simultaneous Thermogravimetric Analysis-Differential Thermal Analysis (TG-DTA, DIAMOND 5700). Samples were heated from 25 to 800 ℃ at a ramp rate of 10 ℃·min⁻¹ under a nitrogen purge (50 mL·min⁻¹). The resulting thermogravimetric profiles were analyzed to assess the onset of thermal degradation and the overall thermal robustness of the materials.
Thermochromic performance and chromatic stability were quantitatively evaluated using a spectrophotometer (DS-220). Optical images of the thermochromic nanofiber membranes were captured across a discrete temperature gradient. CIE L*a*b* coordinates were acquired from multiple spatially distinct regions to derive mean chromaticity parameters and assess spatial homogeneity. To evaluate long-term durability and fatigue resistance, the TC-RGY nanofiber series was subjected to a rigorous thermal cycling regimen (200 cycles spanning the 20-35 °C transition range) utilizing an electronic environmental chamber (CWJ-12L) and a thermostatic stage (BY-S110). Chromatic coordinates (L*, a*, b*) during the transition phase were monitored at specific checkpoints (10, 50, 100, and 200 cycles). The total color difference (ΔE) relative to the pristine state was computed to characterize thermochromic reversibility and resistance to fatigue degradation.
Temperature-responsive spectral reflectance characteristics were elucidated using a UV-Vis-NIR spectrophotometer (UV-3600) equipped with a diffuse reflectance integrating sphere. Spectra were acquired across a wavelength range of 300-800 nm under controlled thermal conditions spanning a gradient of 20-35 °C.
Photothermal modulation performance was evaluated using a simulated solar irradiation system integrated with a xenon light source (CEL-S500-T5), an automated optical power meter (CEL-NP2000-2(10)A), and a multi-channel data logger (SH-16XL). The incident irradiance was calibrated to 1000 W·m⁻² (1 Sun). The standardized testing protocol encompassed three distinct phases: a 5 min pre-irradiation stabilization, a 15 min photothermal heating phase, and a 10 min post-irradiation cooling phase. Temporal temperature profiles were continuously monitored to benchmark the photothermal regulatory efficacy of the different specimens.
To rigorously evaluate the adaptive thermochromic and thermoregulatory efficacy under realistic meteorological conditions, outdoor field testing was conducted using a custom-fabricated experimental apparatus based on a thermally insulating polystyrene foam enclosure. The system was instrumented with four independent measurement units, each containing a thermocouple interface connected to a multi-channel data logger (SH-16XL) for real-time thermal acquisition. Specimens (3 cm × 3 cm) were centrally mounted onto the test slots, directly overlying the temperature probes. To mitigate experimental artifacts arising from convective heat loss and lateral thermal conduction, each unit was sealed with a transparent polyethylene film. Concurrently, solar irradiance was monitored via an automated optical power meter (CEL-NP2000-2(10)A), and ambient temperature/humidity profiles were tracked using a GSP-6 recorder, thereby providing a comprehensive dataset for the correlative analysis of the material's in situ thermal response.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Structural Characterization and Performance Analysis of FPCM
To elucidate the intermolecular interactions and microstructural characteristics within the FPCM, Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analyses were conducted (Figure 4a, b). As depicted in the FTIR spectra (Figure 4a), the retention of characteristic absorption peaks corresponding to PEG1000 (2882 cm⁻¹,1110 cm⁻¹) and PVA (~3322 cm⁻¹) confirms the chemical structural integrity of the constituent polymers. Notably, the hydroxyl (-OH) absorption band of the FPCM exhibits significant broadening and a red-shift relative to pristine PVA. This spectral evolution implies the formation of a robust hydrogen-bonding association network between the PEG1000 chains and the PVA/CNF-COOH/MWCNTs-COOH matrix system.

Figure 4. Structural elucidation and performance metrics of the FPCM: (a) FTIR spectra; (b) XRD patterns; (c) Tensile stress-strain curves; (d) DSC thermograms; (e) Melting/crystallization enthalpies and transition temperatures; (f) TG profiles.
XRD diffractograms further reveal the modulation of crystallization behavior driven by these interactions (Figure 4b). While the FPCM preserves the characteristic diffraction peaks of PEG1000 at 2θ ≈ 19.2° and 23.4°, a marked attenuation in peak intensity is observed. This reduction in crystallinity is attributed to a "dual confinement effect": primarily, the crosslinked PVA network and rigid nanofillers impose significant steric hindrance, while concurrently, enhanced hydrogen bonding anchors the polymer segments. Synergistically, these mechanisms restrict the lattice growth and perfection of PEG1000, resulting in substantial crystal grain refinement.
This synergistic architecture of "physical reinforcement–chemical confinement" endowed the composite membrane with superior mechanical robustness (Figure 4c). The FPCM exhibited characteristic ductile fracture behavior, achieving a tensile strength of 7.2 MPa and an elongation at break of 167%. These results suggest that the high-aspect-ratio CNF-COOH and high-modulus MWCNTs-COOH functioned as a skeletal framework, effectively facilitating load transfer. Concurrently, the glutaraldehyde-induced acetal crosslinking network restricted excessive intermolecular slippage, thereby enhancing structural rigidity without compromising the material's intrinsic flexibility.
Thermal analysis (Figure 4d–f) corroborated the trade-off and optimization of phase change performance resulting from structural encapsulation. DSC thermograms (Figure 4d, e) indicated that the FPCM retained a reversible, single phase-transition peak, with a measured melting enthalpy (ΔHm) of 59.14 J·g⁻¹. Although this value falls below the theoretical calculation (representing approximately 70.4% of the theoretical capacity) due to the aforementioned chain segment confinement effect, it nevertheless ensures a substantial energy storage density. Correspondingly, TG profiles (Figure 4f) revealed that the onset temperature of thermal decomposition for the FPCM was delayed relative to pure PEG1000, exhibiting a multi-stage weight loss characteristic. This enhanced thermal stability stems from the barrier effect established by the crosslinked network and carbon-based fillers; the char layer formed at elevated temperatures effectively retarded heat transfer and the diffusion of volatile decomposition products, thereby validating the efficacy of the FPCM as an encapsulation matrix in suppressing leakage and elevating thermal safety.
Figure 5 illustrates the macroscopic morphology and flexibility characteristics of the FPCM. The synthesized composite membrane manifests a continuous, dense, and uniformly black texture, corroborating the homogeneous dispersion of MWCNTs-COOH throughout the matrix. Notably, the membrane demonstrates exceptional mechanical compliance, capable of withstanding rigorous deformations—including high-curvature bending, twisting, and intricate folding (exemplified by the "paper boat" origami configuration)—without exhibiting any macroscopic fracturing or delamination. This intrinsic flexibility is attributed to a dual-structural mechanism: primarily, the interpenetrating network constructed by PVA and CNF-COOH establishes a continuous scaffold for efficient load transfer; secondarily, the plasticizing effect of glycerol effectively attenuates strong inter-segmental interactions. This facilitates stress dissipation via chain segment slippage and synergistic rearrangement under macroscopic strain, thereby precluding brittle failure.

To rigorously assess shape stability during phase transitions, specimens were subjected to an accelerated thermal leakage test (80 ℃，2 h，, significantly surpassing the melting point of PEG1000). As detailed in Table 1, the average mass loss rate across five parallel replicates was merely 2.06% (range: 1.97%-2.12%). Post-treatment, the samples retained their integral film morphology with no discernible liquid exudation. This superior shape-stabilization capability validates the efficacy of our hierarchical encapsulation strategy: the glutaraldehyde-induced chemically crosslinked network provides macroscopic physical confinement, while the micro/nano-porous architecture within the CNF-COOH/MWCNTs-COOH scaffold exerts potent capillary forces and interfacial adsorption. Synergistically, these mechanisms immobilize the molten PEG1000, effectively restricting its flow and migration.
In summary, the FPCM fabricated via solution casting and in situ crosslinking successfully reconciles the intrinsic trade-off between "rigidity" and "leakage" prevalent in conventional PCMs. The material achieves efficient encapsulation (leakage rate < 2.1%) via the PVA/CNF/CNT scaffold while simultaneously preserving exceptional mechanical flexibility (elongation at break ≈ 167%). This critical structural-performance balance between "flexible deformation" and "phase-change thermal storage" establishes a robust experimental foundation for the development of flexible thermal management devices adaptable to complex geometric surfaces.
Microstructural Topography of PVDF/TC-RGY Nanofiber Membranes
Figure 6 presents a comparative analysis of the microstructural morphologies and statistical diameter distributions for the pristine PVDF membrane versus the TC-RGY composite series with varying TCMs loadings (10-50 wt%). As illustrated in Figures 6(a) and 6(a1), pristine PVDF fibers exhibit a continuous, smooth cylindrical geometry, self-assembling into a uniform three-dimensional porous network via stochastic stacking. The corresponding diameter distribution is relatively narrow (0.6-1.0 μm), conforming to a standard Gaussian distribution.

Figure 6. SEM morphologies and fiber diameter distributions of PVDF and TC-RGY composite nanofiber membranes with varying TCM loadings: (a) PVDF; (b) TC-RGY10; (c) TC-RGY20; (d) TC-RGY30; (e) TC-RGY40; (f) TC-RGY50; (a1-f1) Corresponding high-magnification morphologies; (a2-f2) Corresponding fiber diameter distribution histograms.
Upon the incorporation of TCMs, pronounced spindle-like nodes emerge along the fiber axis (Figures 6(b)–(f)). The dimensions of these nodes are commensurate with the particle size of the microcapsules, and their distribution density increases monotonically with higher loading fractions. This morphological evolution is ascribed to the synergistic interplay of "physical embedding" and "rheological modulation": the steric occupancy of micron-sized capsules directly induces localized radial expansion of the fibers, while the elevated viscoelasticity resulting from high solid content attenuates the further elongation and thinning of the jet within the electric field.
Quantitative morphometric data further corroborates these trends (Figures 6(b2)-(f2)). As the TCMs loading escalates to 50 wt%, the fiber diameter distribution profile exhibits significant broadening and a rightward shift toward larger dimensions, with the peak center migrating from 0.8 μm (pristine PVDF) to the 1.0-1.2 μm interval (TC-RGY50). Despite the fiber coarsening and surface undulations induced by the filler effect, no evidence of fiber fracture or network collapse was observed. This confirms that the PVDF matrix achieves robust encapsulation and accommodation of the microcapsules, thereby establishing a structural foundation for the functional stability of the thermochromic composite.
Chemical Structural Analysis and Mechanical Property Evaluation of PVDF/TC-RGY Nanofiber Membranes
To elucidate the interaction mechanisms between the Thermochromic Microcapsules (TCMs) and the PVDF matrix, Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to characterize both the raw materials and the TC-RGY composite membranes (Figure 7a, b). As depicted in Figure 7(a), all three TCM variants exhibit distinct stretching vibration bands for -CH2/-CH3 in the 2800-3000 cm⁻¹ region, alongside a characteristic peak at ~ 1740 cm⁻¹ assigned to ester/carbonyl moieties. These spectral signatures confirm the compositional and structural homogeneity of the microcapsule shells across different transition temperatures. In the spectra of the composite membranes (Figure 7b), the characteristic absorption peaks of PVDF (840,880,1072,1274,1402 cm⁻¹) are fully preserved, indicating that the polymeric backbone remained intact throughout the electrospinning process. Concurrently, the intensity of the characteristic TCMs signals (1740 cm⁻¹ and 2800-3000 cm⁻¹) exhibits a linear intensification trend correlating with increasing loading fractions (10-50 wt%), thereby corroborating the successful incorporation of the microcapsules. Notably, despite the attenuation in the relative intensity of the PVDF peaks due to the filler effect, neither peak shifts nor peak broadening were discernible. This observation implies the absence of detectable chemical reactions between the TCMs and the PVDF matrix; consequently, the assembly of the composite system relies predominantly on physical encapsulation and mechanical blending rather than chemical bonding.
While the chemical structure remained stable, the physical incorporation of TCMs exerted a quantifiable impact on the mechanical behavior of the nanofiber membranes (Figures 7c, d). The pristine PVDF membrane exhibited superior ductility (elongation at break > 100%) and tensile strength (5.81 MPa). At low loading levels (TC-RGY10), the mechanical degradation was relatively mild (strength: 4.18 MPa; elongation: ~ 84%), indicating that minor microcapsule inclusions caused only limited disruption to fiber continuity. However, as the loading fraction escalated to 20-50 wt%, a distinct downward shift in the stress-strain curves was observed; notably, for TC-RGY50, the tensile strength and elongation plummeted to 0.89 MPa and below 50%, respectively.

FTIR spectral analysis and tensile properties of TCM precursors and PVDF/TC-RGY nanofiber membranes: (a) FTIR spectra of the three distinct TCMs; (b) FTIR spectra of pristine PVDF and TC-RGY nanofiber membranes with varying TCM loadings (10-50 wt%); (c) Tensile stress-strain curves; (d) Histograms of tensile strength and elongation at break.
This deterioration in mechanical performance is highly consistent with the aforementioned microstructural characteristics. Functioning as a rigid discrete phase, the embedded micron-sized capsules interrupted the axial continuity of the fibers and induced stress concentration. Furthermore, owing to the weak physical interfacial adhesion, interfacial debonding readily occurred under tensile loading, thereby accelerating crack initiation and propagation.
Phase Change Thermal Storage Behavior and Thermal Stability of TC-RGY Nanofiber Membranes
To characterize the thermal responsiveness and stability of the TC-RGY nanofiber series, Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) and Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) were conducted on both the pristine PVDF matrix and the composite membranes, with results presented in Figure 8. As evidenced in Figures 8(a) and 8(b), the pristine PVDF membrane exhibits no discernible endothermic or exothermic peaks within the tested temperature interval (10-60 °C), confirming the absence of measurable phase transitions in this regime. In stark contrast, the TC-RGY series displays multiple distinct endothermic peaks during the heating scan and corresponding exothermic peaks during the cooling scan. Crucially, the temperature ranges of these peaks align precisely with the intrinsic transition temperatures of the three selected TCMs (~22 °C, 28 °C, and 33 °C). This correspondence verifies that the thermal functionality of the TCMs was not compromised by solvent evaporation or the high-voltage stretching forces inherent to the electrospinning process.

Figure 8. Thermal behavior and stability profiles of PVDF and TC-RGY nanofiber membranes with varying TCM loadings: (a) DSC heating thermograms; (b) DSC cooling thermograms; (c) Melting / crystallization enthalpies; (d) Melting/crystallization temperatures; (e) TG curves; (f) DTG curves.
Statistical analysis of the phase change enthalpies is summarized in Figure 8(c). As the TCMs loading increases from 10 wt% to 50 wt%, both the melting and crystallization enthalpies exhibit a monotonic increase. This trend elucidates that the latent heat contribution stems predominantly from the microcapsule phase, and elevating the filler content directly amplifies the effective thermal storage capacity of the nanofiber membranes. Correspondingly, the characteristic melting and crystallization temperatures shown in Figure 8(d) display no systematic drift across varying loading levels; the peak temperatures remain stably distributed around the three preset thermal zones. This stability implies that the PVDF matrix exerts negligible influence on the crystallization-melting thermodynamics of the microcapsule core materials. Synthesizing this with the aforementioned FTIR results—which indicated a composite mechanism dominated by physical encapsulation—it can be inferred that the microcapsules function as relatively independent thermal functional units within the fiber matrix, thereby ensuring predictable and reproducible temperature responsiveness.
The thermal stability of the materials was further assessed via TG/DTG analysis (Figures 8e, f). Pristine PVDF exhibits negligible weight loss until approximately 410 ℃. The subsequent rapid degradation corresponds to the scission of C-H and C-F bonds, reaching a maximum decomposition rate in a higher temperature regime, thereby underscoring its superior thermal robustness. In distinct contrast, the three TCMs variants display a significantly lowered onset decomposition temperature (Tonset), with mass loss initiating at ~ 195℃ and the maximum degradation rate (Tmax) occurring at ~ 340 ℃. This behavior is ascribed to the thermal pyrolysis and volatilization of the organic capsule wall and core components.
The composite membrane, exemplified by TC-RGY20, manifests a characteristic two-stage degradation profile. The primary weight loss stage (200-400 ℃) corresponds predominantly to the thermal decomposition of the microcapsule components, while the secondary rapid degradation stage (centered at ~ 425 ℃) aligns with the pyrolysis of the PVDF matrix. Evidently, while the incorporation of TCMs reduces the overall Tonset of the composite, the initial degradation threshold (~ 195 ℃) remains substantially superior to the operational thermal envelope required for wearable or environmental thermal management applications (typically < 60 ℃). Consequently, this poses no constraints on thermal stability under standard service conditions.
Synthesizing the DSC and TGA findings, it is concluded that the TC-RGY nanofiber series possesses responsive, multi-tiered phase-change thermal storage capabilities within the target temperature regime, while simultaneously maintaining a sufficient thermal stability window to accommodate both post-processing requirements and end-use demands.
Optical Responsiveness and Reversible Thermochromic Cyclic Stability of TC-RGY Nanofiber Membranes
Figure 9(a) chronicles the chromatic evolution of the TC-RGY membrane series during a heating ramp from 20 to 35 °C. All specimens exhibit a characteristic stepwise chromatic transition: at 20 °C, the membranes manifest a basal gray tone; as the temperature ascends to 25 °C, the overall hue shifts to light green; upon further heating to 30 °C, a yellow tone achieves spectral dominance; finally, at 35 °C, the pigmentation fades significantly, rendering the membrane nearly white. This cascaded response aligns with the engineered triggering temperatures of the three adopted TCMs. As the ambient temperature traverses the phase-transition interval of each specific microcapsule type, a fraction of the TCMs switches from a colored to a colorless state. This alters the effective combinatorial profile of the "active chromogenic components" within the system, thereby generating macroscopically distinguishable, hierarchical thermal indicators. Correlatively, samples with higher TCMs loadings display superior chromatic contrast at identical thermal checkpoints. Notably, TC-RGY50 exhibits the most pronounced visual saturation and hierarchical perceptibility, a finding consistent with its elevated microcapsule concentration.

The thermochromic evolution of TC-RGY50 was further quantitatively characterized utilizing the CIE L*a*b* colorimetric system (Figure 9b). As the temperature ascended from 20 °C to 35 °C, the luminosity value (L*) exhibited a substantial increase from 66.32 to 93.03. This trajectory is attributed to the progressive fading of the microcapsules, which unmasks the underlying white PVDF fiber network, thereby enhancing the contribution of scattering and reflection of incident light. The evolution of the a* and b* coordinates maps the dominant stages of hue transition: at 25 °C, a* plummeted to -18.92 (a sharp negative shift from -0.86 at 20 °C), consistent with the intensification of green tones; subsequently, at 30 °C, b* surged to 19.38 (up from 9.06 at 25 °C), signifying a marked increase in the yellow component. By 35 °C, a* approached neutrality (-0.57) and b* receded to 3.73, resulting in a "near-white/pale yellowish" terminal appearance. Consequently, these colorimetric parameters not only corroborate the visually observed Gray to Green to Yellow to Near-white sequence but also provide a traceable quantitative basis for defining the dominant hue within each thermal zone.
Color difference analysis (ΔE) was further employed to evaluate the intensity of thermal responsiveness and surface homogeneity. Figure 9(c) reveals distinct ΔE deviations across the 20-25 °C, 25-30 °C, and 30-35 °C intervals, demonstrating that the system achieves effective hierarchical discrimination within all three preset thermal zones. Surface homogeneity was interrogated via inter-regional color differences (Figure 9(d)); the spatial ΔE values at each temperature point remained at negligible levels (< 0.30). This confirms a consistent macroscopic distribution of microcapsules within the fiber matrix, devoid of perceptible mottling or regional chromatic bias caused by localized agglomeration.

Furthermore, the total color difference (ΔE) relative to the pre-cycling baseline remained consistently negligible (< 0.25). These findings underscore the persistent encapsulation efficacy of the microcapsule shells in protecting the core material. Concurrently, the PVDF fiber network functions as a mechanical scaffold and buffer, effectively mitigating the risks of capsule rupture, core leakage, or irreversible chromatic degradation induced by repetitive thermal stress. Synthesizing the visual color grading, quantitative L*a*b* metrics, and cyclic stability assessments, it is concluded that the TC-RGY nanofiber membranes deliver reproducible, hierarchical temperature-indicating outputs within the target thermal zone, possessing the chromatic retention capability necessary for applications involving repeated thermal stimuli.
Spectral Reflectance Characteristics of PVDF/TC-RGY Nanofiber Membranes
To evaluate the solar radiative response capability of the PVDF/TCMs composite nanofiber membranes under varying thermal environments, UV-Vis-NIR reflectance spectra (300-800 nm) were acquired across a temperature gradient of 20-35 °C (Figure 10). As depicted in Figures 10a-d, the pristine PVDF membrane retained a consistently high relative reflectance independent of temperature variations. In contrast, at 20°C, the TC-RGY series exhibited a global reduction in reflectance, a trend that intensified with increasing TCMs loading (Figure 10a). This phenomenon is ascribed to the significant optical absorption contributed by the microcapsules in their chromogenic state, which effectively attenuates the scattering-based reflectance output of the fibrous network.

Figure 10. Relative reflectance characteristics of PVDF and TC-RGY nanofiber membranes with varying TCM loadings: Spectral reflectance curves at (a) 20 ℃; (b) 25 ℃; (c) 30 ℃; and (d) 35 ℃; (e) Average relative reflectance in the Ultraviolet (UV) and Visible (Vis) regions; (f) Differential in average UV and Vis relative reflectance (ΔR) between 35 ℃ and 20 ℃.
Upon heating, the composite membranes exhibit a spectral evolution consistent with the hierarchical bleaching of the TCMs. At 25 °C (Figure 10b), as the low-temperature-triggered components undergo progressive bleaching, distinct reflectance peaks and plateaus emerge within the visible region, accompanied by a marked elevation in reflectance levels relative to the 20 °C baseline. This trend intensifies at 30 °C (Figure 10c) and extends toward longer wavelengths, reflecting the cessation of optical absorption by an increasing fraction of the chromogenic components. By 35 °C (Figure 10d), when the TCMs have transitioned predominantly into their colorless state, visible light absorption is significantly attenuated, causing the overall reflectance of the composite membrane to surge, approximating the high-reflectance baseline of pristine PVDF.
Figure 10(e) quantifies this behavior through band-averaged reflectance analysis: the mean relative reflectance of the TC-RGY samples in both the UV and Vis spectra increases with rising temperature, demonstrating a positive temperature coefficient of reflectance. Taking TC-RGY50 as a representative exemplar, its mean visible relative reflectance escalates from 49.566% at 20°C to 91.073% at 35 °C, yielding a substantial modulation amplitude (ΔR) of approximately 41.507% (Figure 10f). Furthermore, ΔR correlates positively with TCMs loading, indicating that the concentration of "bleachable absorbers" directly dictates the tunable dynamic range between the low-temperature absorptive state and the high-temperature reflective state.
Collectively, the adaptive photothermal regulation of the TC-RGY membranes is governed by the synergistic superposition of two distinct mechanisms: First, the PVDF nanofiber network constitutes a stable, scattering-dominant reflective background, endowing the material with high reflectivity potential in its bleached state. Second, the TCMs introduce active visible-light absorption channels within the low-temperature chromogenic state, thereby suppressing reflectance and augmenting photothermal harvesting. Conversely, upon heating-induced bleaching, these absorption channels are deactivated, facilitating the rapid restoration of reflectivity. This reversible switching mechanism-characterized by "low-temperature absorption versus high-temperature reflection"-provides the fundamental spectral basis for achieving passive radiative thermal regulation across variable thermal environments.
Dynamic Photothermal Response and Thermoregulatory Performance under Simulated Solar Irradiation
To systematically assess the impact of TCMs loading on photothermal regulation behaviors and screen for the optimal host scaffold for the FPCM composite, dynamic heating-cooling tests were conducted on pristine PVDF, the TC-RGY series, and control cotton fabrics under simulated solar irradiation (Figure 11). Under low-temperature ambient conditions (8 ℃ and 15 ℃), the TC-RGY composite membranes exhibited a pronounced photothermal enhancement effect, characterized by equilibrium plateau temperatures that correlated positively with increasing TCMs content (Figures 11a, b). As quantified in Figure 11(f), the composite membranes yielded a net temperature elevation (ΔT) of approximately 2-4 ℃ relative to pristine PVDF. This heating phenomenon is directly ascribed to the chromogenic state of the TCMs at low temperatures: the high optical absorptivity of the microcapsules effectively augmented the net radiative thermal gain of the membrane matrix. Notably, the temperature elevation capability of high-loading samples exhibited a non-linear saturation trend. This behavior stems principally from the increased surface roughness—specifically the node/bead structures induced by high filler content (refer to Figure 6)—which modulated the optical scattering pathways, leading to diminishing marginal returns in photothermal conversion efficiency.

Figure 11. Dynamic thermal response of PVDF and TC-RGY nanofiber series under simulated solar irradiation: Temperature-time profiles at ambient temperatures of (a) 8 ℃, (b) 15 ℃, (c) 25 ℃, and (d) 35 ℃; (e) Equilibrium surface temperatures of all samples under varying ambient conditions; (f) Steady-state temperature differentials (ΔT) relative to pristine PVDF.
With the elevation of ambient temperature, a distinct paradigm shift in the thermoregulatory mode was observed. In the transitional regime of 25 ℃ (Figure 11c), the thermal differential between the composite membranes and the PVDF control exhibited a marked convergence. Upon further heating to an ambient temperature of 35 ℃ (Figure 11d), the equilibrium temperatures of the TC-RGY samples assimilated to those of pristine PVDF, with ΔT values diminishing to negligible or slightly negative magnitudes (Figure 11f). This evidence underscores a critical mechanism: in the fully bleached state, the optical response of the material transitions from an "absorption-dominated" regime to a "reflection/scattering-dominated" regime. Consequently, this enables an effective "insulation and temperature suppression" function under high-temperature operating conditions, thereby mitigating the risk of overheating.
Synthesizing the considerations of photothermal modulation amplitude, structural integrity, and mechanical processability, TC-RGY20 was designated as the optimal host scaffold for the subsequent interlayer integration. Functionally, this specimen successfully realized a bidirectional adaptive regulation mechanism: "low-temperature photothermal heating" versus "high-temperature reflective suppression." Structurally and mechanically, relative to counterparts with higher TCMs loadings, TC-RGY20 exhibited a superior equilibrium in preserving nanofiber network continuity (Figure 6) while mitigating mechanical property degradation (Figures 7c, d). This distinct capability to reconcile thermal management efficacy with structural reliability establishes a robust experimental foundation for the construction of high-performance, thermochromic adaptive composite systems.
Structural Characterization of the TC-RGY20@FPCM Adaptive Thermochromic Composite Membrane
Figure 12 elucidates the comparative surface and cross-sectional micro-morphologies of the TC-RGY20 layer pre- and post-integration, thereby unveiling the implications of the composite assembly protocol on both the fibrous architecture and the interlayer interface. As depicted in Figures 12(a1) and 12(a2), the pristine TC-RGY20 membrane exhibits the quintessential topology associated with electrospinning. The PVDF fibers are stochastically deposited to construct a highly interconnected, three-dimensional porous network. Notably, distinct spindle-like nodes are discernible along the fiber axes, a morphological feature induced by the physical encapsulation and steric accommodation of the TCMs within the fiber matrix.
Post-integration (Figures 12b1, b2), the fibrous network architecture on the TC-RGY20 side retained its pristine integrity, appearing devoid of any fiber coalescence, structural collapse, or encapsulation by a dense adhesive film. This remarkable morphological stability is principally attributed to the mild lamination parameters employed (40 ℃, 0.05 MPa). Specifically, the processing temperature—maintained significantly below the melting point of PVDF—effectively precluded thermal softening of the polymer scaffold. Concurrently, the low contact pressure facilitated robust interfacial adhesion without compromising the intrinsic open-porous architecture. The preservation of this porosity is pivotal for functional efficacy. It serves a dual purpose by minimizing optical occlusion of the microcapsules' chromogenic transitions by the binder, while simultaneously ensuring the substantial contribution of the fibrous interface to efficient light scattering.

Cross-sectional SEM analysis (Figures 12c1, c2) further substantiates the efficacy of the interlayer integration strategy. This physical "interlocking anchorage" not only enhances the interfacial shear strength but also effectively minimizes thermal contact resistance. Collectively, this robustly integrated yet structurally distinct laminar configuration establishes a solid microstructural foundation for the coupled response of "phase-change thermoregulation" and "thermochromic indication".
Evaluation of Thermoregulatory Performance of TC-RGY20@FPCM Composite Membranes under Outdoor Solar Irradiation
To rigorously assess the in-situ thermal response of the TC-RGY20@FPCM adaptive composite membrane under realistic outdoor boundary conditions, a custom-engineered experimental platform anchored by a thermally insulating expanded polystyrene (EPS) chamber was constructed and deployed in an open-field environment (Figures 13a, b). In the experimental protocol, a bare copper foil was designated as the reference baseline (representing a surface devoid of specific thermal management capabilities). Pristine PVDF and TC-RGY20 membranes served as control groups, while the TC-RGY20@FPCM constituted the target specimen. Surface temperatures of all substrates were monitored in real-time via thermocouples, with the synchronized acquisition of total solar irradiance and ambient meteorological parameters (temperature and relative humidity), as illustrated in Figures 13c, d, g, and h.
Under the high-irradiance regime (Figure 13c), the solar flux exhibited intense fluctuations with peak intensities exceeding 600 W/m², concurrent with elevated ambient temperatures (Figure 13g). The surface temperature of the bare copper foil ascended rapidly, mirroring the irradiance profile with pronounced transient oscillations, thereby underscoring the rapid thermal response characteristic of metallic surfaces to incident radiation. In stark contrast, the TC-RGY20@FPCM composite maintained the lowest overall thermal profile, exhibiting a significantly dampened sensitivity to the instantaneous fluctuations in solar flux. Quantifying this performance via the temperature differential ΔT=TCu−Tsample (Figure 13e), the composite membrane achieved a substantial ΔT of approximately 12-17 ℃ during the initial phase, vastly outperforming the pristine PVDF ( ~ 2 ℃).
This disparity aligns with the synergistic bilayer mechanism: as the ambient temperature traversed the bleaching interval of the microcapsules, the TC-RGY20 layer transitioned from a chromogenic state to a highly reflective state. This transition augmented the scattering-based reflectance of the fiber network, thereby mitigating net radiative absorption. Concurrently, the phase-change process of PEG1000 within the FPCM layer absorbed incoming thermal energy via latent heat, effectively buffering thermal spikes. As the test progressed, a slight attenuation in ΔT was observed. This evolution is plausibly attributed to the phase-change process approaching a dynamic thermal equilibrium, alongside the superposition of continuously variable external irradiation and convective boundary conditions. Notwithstanding this, the composite membrane sustained a comparatively low surface temperature throughout the high-irradiance period, demonstrating a robust temperature suppression capability under "intense radiation–high temperature" environmental conditions.

Figure 13. Outdoor Thermoregulatory Performance Assessment: (a) Optical photograph of the experimental setup; (b) Schematic illustration of the temperature measurement apparatus and sample arrangement; (c) Real-time temporal evolution of surface temperatures and solar irradiance under high-irradiance conditions; (d) Real-time temporal evolution of surface temperatures and solar irradiance under low-irradiance conditions; (e, f) Temperature differentials (ΔT=TCu−Tsample) between the copper reference and respective samples corresponding to scenarios (c) and (d); (g, h) Ambient temperature and relative humidity profiles for the two respective irradiation scenarios.
Under the low-irradiance regime (Figure 13d), the solar flux was attenuated to below 200 W·m⁻² with minimal fluctuations, accompanied by a reduced ambient thermal baseline of 16-18 ℃ (Figure 13h). Consequently, the thermoregulatory objective transitioned from high-temperature suppression to the maximization of radiative harvesting to elevate surface temperature. Differential temperature analysis (Figure 13f) reveals that pristine PVDF predominantly exhibited positive ΔT values (implying surface temperatures inferior to the copper reference). This observation aligns with its intrinsic high-reflectivity/scattering nature: under weak irradiation, high reflectance precludes efficient photon uptake, rendering the material unfavorable for thermal accumulation. In distinct contrast, both TC-RGY20 and TC-RGY20@FPCM yielded negative ΔT values (ranging from −2 to −4 ℃), indicating surface temperatures surpassing the copper reference by 2-4 ℃
This "passive heating" phenomenon is mechanistically ascribed to the chromogenic state of the microcapsules at low temperatures: the activation of visible-light absorption channels effectively enhances the net radiative absorption cross-section, thereby generating measurable photothermal gains even under minimal irradiation. It is imperative to note that these findings corroborate the optical evidence regarding the reversible "low-temperature absorption / high-temperature reflection" switching mechanism presented in Figures 9 and 10. Furthermore, the integration of FPCM did not confer significant additional heating advantages within this specific low-irradiance window. This suggests that the primary contribution of the phase-change layer is functionally distinct, manifesting predominantly as thermal buffering during high-flux scenarios or when temperatures traverse the phase-transition interval.
In conclusion, the TC-RGY20@FPCM exhibited bi-directional thermoregulatory outputs tailored to distinct outdoor boundary regimes. Under high-irradiance conditions, the composite realized pronounced temperature suppression through the synergistic superposition of enhanced reflectivity and phase-change thermal buffering. Conversely, under low-irradiance conditions, it facilitated passive thermal gain by leveraging the enhanced absorption cross-section of the chromogenic state. This coupled response mechanism—defined by the environmentally adaptive switching between absorption/reflection optical modes augmented by latent heat buffering—provides robust empirical substantiation for adaptive thermal management in dynamic and complex climatic environments, thereby underscoring the material's substantial energy-efficiency potential and broad practical utility.
CONCLUSIONS
Addressing the intrinsic incompatibility often encountered in personal thermal management (PTM) materials—specifically the difficulty in synergizing environmental adaptive regulation, temperature visualization, and latent heat energy storage—this study engineered and validated a flexible bilayer composite membrane, TC-RGY20@FPCM, featuring a coupled "photo-thermo-chromic-phase change" mechanism. This architecture realizes a distinct laminar heterostructure comprising a "dense phase-change thermal storage layer" and a "porous fibrous regulatory layer." Crucially, robust interlayer adhesion and reproducible thermal response outputs were achieved under mild lamination conditions.
Regarding the design of the Phase Change Material (FPCM) layer, the network confinement established by the crosslinked PVA backbone and CNF-COOH effectively suppressed the migration of molten PEG1000. An average leakage rate of merely 2.06% attests to the composite system's acceptable shape stability. The measured melting enthalpy (ΔHm) of the FPCM was 59.14 J·g⁻¹, representing approximately 70% of the theoretical value. This observed trade-off between "latent heat retention" and "structural confinement" is mechanistically consistent with the aforementioned low-leakage characteristic.
In the construction of the thermochromic regulatory layer (TC-RGY), the influence of thermochromic microcapsule (TCMs) loading on morphology, mechanical properties, and optical behavior was systematically evaluated. Among the variants, TC-RGY20 (20 wt% loading) exhibited the most favorable trade-off between structural integrity and mechanical serviceability. The TC-RGY series manifested stepwise, reversible chromatic transitions within the 20-35 ℃ range. Spectral reflectance analysis further substantiated the reversible "absorption/reflection" switching  mechanism: the composite membrane exhibited diminished visible-light reflectance in the low-temperature chromogenic state, followed by a restoration of reflectivity upon transitioning to the high-temperature bleached state. This behavior aligns precisely with the design rationale of "low-temperature absorption versus high-temperature reflection," thereby establishing the fundamental optical basis for the ensuing photothermal response.
Regarding the interfacial integration between the porous fibrous stratum and the dense phase-change matrix, a TPU-assisted electrostatic electrospraying technique was employed to facilitate interlayer adhesion. Cross-sectional SEM analysis revealed a distinct, well-defined interface devoid of penetrating voids, with the intrinsic porosity of the fibrous layer effectively preserved post-lamination. This implies that the adhesive establishes a robust connection predominantly via a "localized infiltration–mechanical interlocking" mechanism, rather than by forming a continuous, occlusive film on the surface.
In terms of functional validation, in-situ outdoor field testing corroborated the superior environmental adaptability of the TC-RGY20@FPCM. Under high-irradiance regimes, the composite achieved a substantial temperature differential (ΔT) of 12-17 ℃ relative to the copper reference, exemplifying the synergistic superposition of enhanced reflectivity and phase-change thermal buffering. Conversely, under low-irradiance, low-temperature conditions, the surface temperature surpassed that of the copper reference by 2-4 ℃, a finding mechanistically consistent with the enhanced radiative absorption inherent to the low-temperature chromogenic state.
In summary, this study proposes and demonstrates an intelligent passive thermal management strategy predicated on multi-mechanism coupling. This approach successfully orchestrates the synergy among the optical responsiveness of the thermochromic fibrous stratum, the latent heat buffering of the phase-change domain, and the requisite interfacial heat transfer/adhesion properties within a unitary system. This strategy establishes a verifiable structural and performance foundation for the development of flexible thermal management materials endowed with environmental perceptivity, passive regulation, and wearable compatibility. Moreover, it offers a transferable material and methodological paradigm for the design of energy-efficient thermal management architectures, applicable to both next-generation wearable textiles and building envelopes.
